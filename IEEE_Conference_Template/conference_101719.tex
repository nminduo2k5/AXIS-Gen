\begin{abstract}
Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC), standardized as XACML, is widely
adopted in Industrial Control Systems (ICS/OT) under IEC~62443 FR2 Use
Control. Authoring mistakes in XACML policies routinely produce
unauthorized-access or denial-of-service faults, yet testing these policies
at scale is both labor-intensive and methodologically challenging. Existing
mutation-testing and combinatorial-generation techniques are syntactically
rigorous but semantically blind to industrial attribute semantics. Large
Language Models (LLMs) offer domain-aware reasoning but lack formal coverage
guarantees and exhibit a documented permissiveness bias when generating
access-control artifacts. We propose AXIS-Gen, an AI-native test generation
framework combining structured, coverage-explicit LLM prompting with a
lightweight symbolic conflict-witness pass and a real PDP-based mutation oracle.
On a representative ICS water-treatment-plant ABAC policy (6~rules, 51~mutants,
7~mutation operators, 5~seeds, equal 40-request budget), our approach achieves
a mean mutation score of 94.1\% and 100\% rule coverage versus 53.3\%/53.3\%
for random and 56.9\%/60.0\% for pairwise-combinatorial baselines, a 76.5\%
relative improvement. Adding the symbolic pass raises the score to 96.1\%,
closing the combining-algorithm coverage gap that prompting alone cannot address.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Context and Stakes}

ICS/OT environments are increasingly interconnected under Industry~4.0 and
cloud-assisted SCADA, and are actively targeted by cyberattacks.
IEC~62443 FR2 ``Use Control''~\cite{b10} mandates fine-grained,
attribute-based access decisions: \textit{who} (subject role, clearance,
location) may do \textit{what} (action) on \textit{which resource} (type,
zone, criticality) under \textit{what conditions} (safety state, plant mode).
A policy fault here is not merely a data breach --- it can mean a vendor
reconfiguring a safety PLC without authorization, or a cleared engineer unable
to override a stuck interlock during a maintenance window, both with potential
physical consequences~\cite{b19,b20}.

\subsection{The Testing Gap}

XACML's expressiveness makes policy authoring error-prone. Two research streams
exist but operate independently:

\textit{Stream A --- Mutation testing / coverage-based generation}
\cite{b1,b2,b3,b4,b5}: syntactically rigorous (MC/DC, rule-pair coverage,
Z3-based strong mutation) but entirely blind to industrial attribute semantics
that make certain combinations operationally meaningful or adversarially
dangerous.

\textit{Stream B --- LLM-guided test synthesis} \cite{b11,b12,b13,b14,b15}:
domain-aware, but lacking formal coverage guarantees; a 2025 study \cite{b12}
documents significant permissiveness bias (45.8\%--93.7\% semantic equivalence
depending on model). No published work applies LLMs to XACML request synthesis
for ICS environments.

\textit{Gap:} No existing approach combines LLM-level domain reasoning with
the combinatorial rigor that industrial ABAC policies require. This paper fills
that gap.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{AXIS-Gen framework}: LLM synthesis + schema repair + symbolic
    conflict-witness pass + PDP oracle, producing genuine OASIS XACML~3.0 XML
    artifacts.
  \item \textbf{Empirical finding (RQ3)}: combining-algorithm faults are a
    systematic blind spot of any sampling-only LLM approach; root-cause
    analysis and fix via symbolic pass.
  \item \textbf{Oracle design insight}: default-deny catch-all rules under
    \texttt{deny-overrides} mask nearly all mutants --- a first-class
    methodological contribution for any XACML mutation study.
  \item \textbf{Reference implementation} (Python~3.12, reproducible with fixed
    seeds, runtime $\approx$210~s) with 5~figures and 4~tables of results.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{XACML and ICS Security}

XACML~3.0~\cite{b9} provides a policy language (Rule/Policy/PolicySet), a
reference architecture (PEP/PDP/PAP/PIP), and three combining algorithms:
\texttt{deny-overrides}, \texttt{permit-overrides}, \texttt{first-applicable}.
Under IEC~62443 zones/conduits and security levels (SL1--SL4), ABAC/XACML
naturally expresses multi-axis access constraints. Production-grade open-source
PDPs include AuthzForce~\cite{b17} and WSO2 Balana~\cite{b18}.

\subsection{LLMs for Test Synthesis and Access Control}

Dorfer et al.~\cite{b11} apply structured LLM prompting to IEC~61131-3 test
generation without fine-tuning --- the closest published analog to our work.
LLM permissiveness bias in policy synthesis~\cite{b12} motivates our design
decision to use LLMs only as request proposers, never as policy authors or
oracles. LACE~\cite{b13} shows a repair layer is essential. Fuzz4All
\cite{b14} and ChatAFL~\cite{b15} confirm LLM-guided synthesis scales when
paired with a validity/coverage signal.

\textit{Positioning:} This paper is the first to combine LLM-guided test
synthesis with a principled XACML fault model and a symbolic conflict-witness
pass targeting industrial ABAC policies.

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Formal Policy Model}

A policy $P = \langle R_1, \ldots, R_n, \mathit{alg} \rangle$ where
$\mathit{alg} \in \{\texttt{deny-overrides}, \texttt{permit-overrides},
\texttt{first-applicable}\}$. Each rule $R_i = \langle \mathit{effect}_i,
\mathit{target}_i, \mathit{condition}_i \rangle$ where target and condition are
Boolean expressions over atomic predicates (\textit{category.attribute op
value}). The total attribute space $|D| = \prod_a |D_a| = 92{,}160$ for the
reference policy (9 attributes $\times$ finite domains).

\subsection{Oracle Design: The Default-Deny Masking Pitfall}

A default-deny catch-all rule under \texttt{deny-overrides} makes the
top-level decision constant (always DENY), masking nearly all mutants.
This paper deliberately omits this rule to keep NOT\_APPLICABLE observable ---
a methodological contribution applicable to any XACML mutation study.

\section{AI-Native Test Generation Framework}

\subsection{Architecture Overview}

AXIS-Gen operates in five steps. \textit{Design principle:} the LLM is used
only as a domain-aware request proposer --- never as a policy author or oracle.
The PDP is the sole ground-truth authority, directly addressing the
permissiveness bias documented in~\cite{b12}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Policy Ingestion \& NL Rendering}: parse XACML; build
    natural-language rule descriptions and attribute domain table.
  \item \textbf{LLM Prompt \& Generation}: structured prompt with 4 coverage
    objectives $\to$ JSON candidate requests.
  \item \textbf{Schema/Domain Repair}: clamp out-of-domain values; retry on
    malformed JSON ($\leq$3 times).
  \item \textbf{Symbolic Conflict-Witness Pass}: CSP/brute-force over finite
    domain ($<$1~s) --- guaranteed rule-pair coverage.
  \item \textbf{PDP Oracle + Mutation Scoring}: ground-truth decisions; compute
    mutation score and coverage metrics.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Component 2: Structured LLM Prompt}

The system prompt prioritizes 4 objectives in explicit order, each mapped to a
known coverage criterion (Table~\ref{tab:prompt}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{LLM Prompt Objectives and Coverage Criterion Mappings}
\label{tab:prompt}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|p{1.8cm}|p{1.5cm}|p{1.3cm}|}
\hline
\textbf{P} & \textbf{Objective} & \textbf{Criterion} & \textbf{Kills} \\
\hline
1 & $\geq$1 request per rule & Rule Coverage \cite{b4} & MRD \\
\hline
2 & Flip each predicate, others fixed & MC/DC \cite{b4,b6} & CPM, CEM \\
\hline
3 & Values $n{-}1$, $n$, $n{+}1$ for thresholds & Boundary Value & CVM \\
\hline
4 & $\geq$1 request satisfying both targets of each rule-pair & Rule-Pair \cite{b4} & RCM \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{table}

Output format: JSON array only, no prose. Schema enforces
\texttt{``category.attribute''} keys with values drawn exclusively from the
declared finite domain.

\subsection{Component 4: Symbolic Conflict-Witness Pass}

A \textit{conflict witness} for rule-pair $(R_i, R_j)$ is a request $r$ such
that both $R_i.\mathit{target}(r) \wedge R_i.\mathit{condition}(r)$ and
$R_j.\mathit{target}(r) \wedge R_j.\mathit{condition}(r)$ evaluate to true.
This is required to kill RCM mutants --- only when $\geq$2 rules fire
simultaneously does the combining algorithm determine the final decision.

\textit{Why prompting alone is insufficient for Objective~4:} finding a genuine
conflict witness is a constraint-satisfaction problem. Sampling merely hopes
to land in the right region --- confirmed empirically in
Section~\ref{sec:rq3}. The prototype uses brute-force enumeration over
$|D|{=}92{,}160$ points; a production implementation would use Z3 SMT
solver~\cite{b3} for continuous/high-cardinality attributes.

\textbf{RQ1 (Effectiveness):} LLM-Guided achieves 94.1\% mutation score
vs.~53.3\% Random (+76.5\% relative improvement) and 56.9\% Pairwise
(+65.5\%). Rule coverage reaches 100\% in all 5~seeds with zero variance
vs.~53.3\% for Random. Hybrid raises the score to 96.1\%.

\textbf{RQ2 (Efficiency):} LLM-Guided reaches the 20-kill threshold in
5.6~requests on average vs.~16.0 for Random (2.86$\times$ gain) and 13.2 for
Pairwise (2.36$\times$ gain). Rule-targeting front-loads the most fault-revealing
requests.

\subsection{Combining-Algorithm Blind Spot (RQ3)}
\label{sec:rq3}

At budget~300, LLM-Guided kills 49/51 mutants. The 2~survivors are both RCM:
\texttt{RCM\_permit-overrides} and \texttt{RCM\_first-applicable}.

\textbf{Root cause:} killing an RCM mutant requires $\geq$2 rules to fire
simultaneously. The per-rule activation strategy (LLM objectives~1--3) does
not systematically construct multi-rule conflict witnesses --- this is a CSP,
not a sampling problem.

Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap} shows 100\% kill fraction on all other operator types
but 0\% on RCM --- a sharp, systematic gap.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig5_operator_heatmap.png}}
\caption{Heatmap of killed mutants per operator type. RCM shows 0\% kill fraction
         for LLM-Guided (without symbolic pass), confirming the combining-algorithm
         blind spot.}
\label{fig:heatmap}
\end{figure}

This RQ3 finding is the key empirical contribution: it explains why structured
prompting alone is insufficient and why the symbolic pass is
\textit{architecturally necessary} --- not just incrementally helpful.

\subsection{Hybrid Ablation (RQ4)}

Adding the symbolic conflict-witness pass kills 1 additional RCM mutant
(94.1\%~$\to$~96.1\%). The remaining survivor is a likely \textit{equivalent
mutant}: under this policy structure, both \texttt{permit-overrides} and
\texttt{first-applicable} produce identical decisions on all 92,160~input points.

\textit{Trade-off:} Req$\to$20-killed increases slightly (5.6~$\to$~6.8)
because symbolic probes front-load for rare RCM faults. A production
implementation should interleave symbolic probes by estimated fault likelihood.

\subsection{Research Integrity Note}

All results use \texttt{HeuristicSurrogateLLMClient} --- a deterministic
program executing the same 4-objective prompt strategy without a language model
call. This validates the \textit{strategy}, not a specific model. Real-LLM
clients (Claude, GPT, Gemini) are implemented; replacing the surrogate requires
one configuration change and an API key. The numbers validate:
\textit{structured, coverage-explicit prompting $\gg$ random/combinatorial
sampling}. Real-LLM evaluation is Study~1 in our roadmap
(Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).

\section{Threats to Validity}

\textbf{Construct validity:} The surrogate validates the prompting strategy,
not a deployed LLM. The prototype PDP covers XACML~3.0 core but omits
obligations/advice, multi-value bag semantics, and XPath categories. A full
study should use AuthzForce~\cite{b17} or Balana~\cite{b18} as the production oracle.

\textbf{Internal validity:} Fixed random seeds and a deterministic surrogate
guarantee exact reproducibility. Score variation across 5 seeds is low:
$\pm$3.8\,pp (Random), $\pm$5.9\,pp (Pairwise), $\pm$2.1\,pp (LLM variants).

\textbf{External validity:} A single 6-rule policy cannot establish
generalization across policy sizes, combining algorithms, or attribute-domain
cardinalities. Study~3 (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}) addresses this with a
benchmark corpus.

\textbf{LLM permissiveness bias (open):} LLMs may systematically under-sample
deny-triggering or safety-critical attribute combinations --- the worst failure
mode for a security test generator. This is the first-priority question
in Study~2.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented AXIS-Gen, an AI-native test generation framework for industrial
XACML access-control policies. On a realistic 6-rule ICS policy with 51~mutants
and 7~mutation operators, our LLM-guided approach achieves 94.1\% mutation score
and 100\% rule coverage in 5.6~requests on average --- a 76.5\% relative
improvement in fault detection and a 2.86$\times$ efficiency gain over random
at equal request budget. The symbolic conflict-witness pass raises the score to
96.1\% by closing the combining-algorithm coverage gap that LLM prompting alone
cannot address.

Three takeaways for practitioners: (1)~coverage-explicit, structured LLM
prompting substantially outperforms undirected sampling for ABAC policy testing;
(2)~combining-algorithm coverage requires a constraint-solving step, not just
sampling; (3)~oracle design --- specifically avoiding default-deny masking ---
matters as much as request generation.
